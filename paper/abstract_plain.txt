Wearable and phone-based monitoring often raises an alarm when a person's recent behaviour departs from that person's own baseline. Scaling the alarm by each person's own variability is meant to make such rules fair, but we found no evaluation of whether people with steady and variable routines are in fact treated equally. We audit four standard detectors (a fixed threshold, a personal z-score, EWMA and CUSUM) on simulated daily step counts. Each detector is tuned to the same pooled alarm rate, and the lowest and highest terciles of day-to-day variability are compared on alarm rate, restricted mean detection delay and detection within 14 days, against margins set before the first run. The fixed threshold favours variable users: about 3.3 more alarms per 100 user-days and faster detection. The three personal detectors show negligible delay and detection gaps for a change of one personal standard deviation, and alarm-rate gaps below one per 100 user-days in the base simulation. This reverses when equal treatment means the same absolute change in steps: variable users are then detected 2.3 to 3.2 days later. A detector that mixes own and population scale moves between these two views; a balanced mixture exists in the base simulation but is not stable across settings. The absence of a delay gap for personal detectors held in all 20 simulator settings tried. A consistency check on 51 users of the open LifeSnaps data is mostly uninformative, and it shows a CUSUM alarm-rate gap larger than any simulator setting produced, which we could not explain. Code, dated plans and all results are public.
