Monitoring systems for wearables and phones often raise an alarm when a person's behaviour departs from that person's own baseline; we ask whether such alarms treat people with low- and high-variability routines equally. We audit four standard detectors (a fixed threshold, a personal z-score, EWMA and CUSUM) on simulated daily step counts, tune them to the same alarm rate, and compare the highest and lowest variability terciles of users on alarm rate and detection delay under two definitions of equal treatment: the same change in personal standard deviations, or the same change in steps. In simulation, the fixed threshold gives high-variability users about 3.3 more alarms per 100 user-days but faster detection; the personal detectors show no delay gap beyond our margin for a change of one personal standard deviation in any of 20 settings, but detect a fixed change in steps 2.3 to 3.2 days later in high-variability users, so no single scale meets both definitions. These findings rest on a simulator whose assumptions we chose; a check on 51 users of the public LifeSnaps data (17 per group) is mostly inconclusive and shows a CUSUM alarm-rate gap larger than any simulated setting, which we could not explain. Code, analysis plans and all results are public.
